Local organizations assist People’s City Mission during winter months

By Mackenzie Evans

Lincoln, Neb -- Many places in Lincoln served as warming centers in winter.

As homelessness increases in Lincoln, so does the need for shelter.

Homelessness in Lincoln increased from 320 people in 2021 to 542 people in 2025, according to UNL's Center on Children, Families, and the Law. In January, that number increased to 594.

This increase follows state and national trends, according to the United States Department of Housing and Urban Development. Between 2021 and 2024, Nebraska saw an increase from 1,935 to 2,720 individuals experiencing homelessness. The United States overall saw the total number more than double from 380,630 to 771,480. 

Brittany Brakenhoff, a research assistant professor at UNL’s Center on Children, Families, and the Law, explained how the Homeless Point-in-Time count was collected in Lincoln in January.

“It’s like a literal count where volunteers go out on the streets to try to find people who are unsheltered, and then the shelter gives a count of how many people are staying there that night,” Brakenhoff said.

Brakenhoff said Homeless Point-in-time counts are federally required by the Department of Housing and Urban Development to be conducted by every community.

Brakenhoff said one reason the rate has increased over the past five years may be that relief funds issued to assist with housing during COVID are no longer available.

Amy Pappas, the senior vice president of operations at the People’s City Mission, spoke about how rising homelessness rates affect Lincoln’s only homeless shelter.

Pappas said the shelter was designed to accommodate 250 people. However, even before the first snowstorm of the season in November, the shelter was regularly hosting 400 people. That number increased to 443 during the coldest nights of January.

“We've never had that many people,” Pappas said.

So far, the shelter has been able to help everyone who comes in for assistance. This has been accomplished by doubling the number of families in rooms and adding mattresses to bunkhouse floors, lounge areas and the facility’s daycare space.

Pappas said residents who were previously banned for behavior were allowed to return once the temperature dropped below freezing. The shelter placed these residents on mattresses in the lobby so that staff could keep an eye on them and to keep other residents safe.

Pappas said the women’s shelter in particular has more people than ever before, while the men’s shelter remains closer to average.

The People’s City Mission plans to begin construction on additional shelter space in April of 2027, which would increase the shelter’s capacity by 300 additional residents. Donors are funding the project. The shelter is on a flood plain, so dirt will need to be packed down before construction can begin.

The new shelter building will reduce overcrowding. Until construction is complete, the People’s City Mission is seeking other options to meet the increased demand for shelter. In November of 2025, the shelter reached out to churches and the Lincoln-Lancaster County Health Department to find warming centers.

According to the Lincoln-Lancaster County Health Department, many organizations in Lincoln serve as warming centers during the coldest days of winter.

According to the health department, warming centers are heated buildings that allow people to come in during cold days. These include Lincoln City Libraries and Matt Talbot. 

Lincoln’s City Libraries also provide warm and quiet spaces for people without shelter or heating on cold days.

For safety reasons, patrons are not allowed to sleep in the library. 

Unsheltered individuals may come into the library during the day, then go to the People’s City Mission at night.

Amy Huffman, a public information specialist at Lincoln City Libraries, said that libraries are one of the few public places where people can come in and not have to spend money.

“Unofficially, public libraries around the country have always served as warming and cooling centers,” Huffman said.

In January, the Lincoln-Lancaster County Health Department officially designated the Lincoln City Libraries as warming centers.

Huffman said the library tries to stay open as much as possible during the winter. Even as schools close, librarians continue to read to children as scheduled. This provides students who are out of school a warm and safe place to continue learning.

Huffman said that unhoused patrons often wait outside of the Bennet Martin branch of the library on cold mornings, waiting for the doors to open at 10 a.m. She recalled one man who came in with icicles on his beard and expressed gratitude to be let into a warm building.

The library also serves as a place for unhoused patrons to fill out job applications, read and make phone calls. They can also access services through CenterPointe.

CenterPointe is a local healthcare nonprofit that specializes in mental health and substance abuse rehabilitation. They also assist homeless people by providing CenterPlace Apartments within their medical campus, Campus for Health and WellBeing.

Matt Talbot Kitchen and Outreach has also extended its hours during cold, snowy days.

Like the library, Matt Talbot continues to operate as usual during snow days when other organizations may need to close.

Victoria O'Neil, the director of hunger relief and volunteerism at Matt Talbot Kitchen and Outreach, said that before winter arrived, Matt Talbot had already had a busy year. In the summer of 2025, the kitchen had to stop serving seconds due to increased attendance at meal times. SNAP cuts in November further increased the workload.

As temperatures drop, the need for Matt Talbot’s services continues to rise.

Another snowstorm came on Feb. 19.

“We have a full house today,” said Leanne Pelser, the director of housing and outreach programs at Matt Talbot, on the Friday after the snowstorm.

On Thursday night, volunteers from CHI Health St. Elizabeth’s Mission Integration Committee continued to serve meals, even as heavy snowfall made travel difficult. Pelser said the group got stuck in traffic that got backed up from the snow. Although they were late, they kept going and served everyone there.

O’Neil said that Matt Talbot also provides blankets, hats and gloves to those who need them.
